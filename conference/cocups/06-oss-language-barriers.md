# My Broken English — 語言、文化與全球開源協作的摩擦

**Source recordings:** 新錄音 31 & 32
**Speaker:** **Naruhiko Ogasawara** (per schedule) — Japanese open-source developer
**COSCUP session:** 「My broken English…」, Room TR410, Day 1
**Topic tags:** Non-native English speakers, open-source collaboration, documentation culture, GitHub vs. Subversion, cross-cultural communication

## One-line summary

A candid, personal reflection from a Japanese developer on the friction non-native English
speakers face contributing to global open source — how he taught himself English, why
documentation gaps are really *culture* gaps, and how native/non-native speakers split apart in
international meetings.

> This is a **experience/reflection talk**, not a technical one. There are no repos or tools to
> track down — the value is the lived account. The transcript is heavily disfluent (code-switched
> English/Japanese, ASR-mangled), so the notes below reconstruct the argument rather than quote.

## The core argument

For an Asian, non-native English developer, the hardest thing about open source isn't the code —
it's **expressing your project to the world in a common language**. As he puts it in Japanese,
*"that's the hardest part."*

## How he learned English (three self-taught methods)

1. **Drawing/illustration to the point of exhaustion** — learned by making visual material relentlessly ("死ぬほど学んだ").
2. **Asking a translation team to teach him** — and being firmly told: *never* just paste in program output / raw code as a substitute for explaining. Explain in words.
3. **Online gaming with foreigners** — endless conversation with non-Japanese players. This taught him a regional lesson (below).

## The West-Coast / East-Coast observation

A recurring, half-joking theme:

- **West Coast (San Francisco-area) people** — relaxed about English; you can speak messily and it's fine. "They don't think much about English," so no problem talking casually.
- **East Coast (New York) people** — expect you to speak seriously/properly, or they genuinely get annoyed. He notes he "often gets scolded" there.
- He mentions a **New York-born participant** in the same MCP/CROSS session who was reportedly frustrated by Japanese speakers' English when first arriving in Japan.
- **Argentina** people and others were friendly and easy to speak with; "some guys from the east US" were the hard ones.

## Documentation gaps are culture gaps

The deepest point of the talk:

- He repeatedly asked project maintainers to explain a **feature's mechanism** so he could integrate it into his own open-source process — and was **refused several times**. Not out of malice: the other party wouldn't (or couldn't) document it.
- This was in the **Subversion era**, before GitHub — so there was no easy shared mechanism to point at. He asked "I don't understand this mechanism, please teach me" multiple times and never got an answer, so he **couldn't add the feature**.
- His conclusion: these blockers ultimately **trace back to cultural difference**. When both sides are non-native and cultures differ, communication breaks down — and some people, even after open-sourcing code, keep working in a closed way and won't leave documentation.
- His own stance: *真正的* open source means **accepting many people's input**. Even without GitHub back then, he published his own work properly as open source, welcomed any comments, and wanted to spread it widely — because open source is about taking in everyone's contributions.

## The native / non-native split in global meetings

- In global meetings, the real problem is that **native and non-native speakers cleanly separate** — the conversation bifurcates.
- His ask: native speakers should show **respect** (he stumbles on the word "regression"/"respect" in the recording) toward non-native speakers rather than leaving them behind.
- An aside on **Indian English**: he found it hard to parse at first and had to check carefully what was meant; he also encountered strong pride that made collaboration difficult in specific cases. He explicitly frames these as **exceptions / individual cases**, not a generalization — and says working with genuinely diverse people on open source is simply very hard, but worth it.

## Takeaway for the reader

There's nothing to install here. The session is a reminder that the barriers to global
open-source participation are **linguistic and cultural**, and that "just write documentation"
is easier said than done when documentation itself is a cultural practice not everyone shares.
If you run a project with international contributors, the actionable version of his plea is:
document mechanisms explicitly, and give non-native speakers room and respect in synchronous
discussion.
